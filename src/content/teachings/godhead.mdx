There is one God. Not three persons sharing one substance, and not three gods in agreement — one eternal, invisible Spirit, who has revealed Himself to His creation in three great offices: as Father, as Son, and as Holy Ghost. Scripture traces His path into visibility: the eternal Spirit brought Himself into a form — the Word — and that Word became a man, Jesus Christ. The invisible God now has a face, and He will never have another. Every other teaching on this site stands on this one.

## What the Scripture Says

### "The LORD our God is one LORD"

The foundation of all revelation is Israel's first confession:

<Scripture ref="Deuteronomy 6:4">
  "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD:"
</Scripture>

Jesus Himself, asked which commandment is first of all, began exactly there: "The first of all the commandments is, Hear, O Israel; The Lord our God is one Lord" (Mark 12:29). And the prophets leave the confession no loophole. "I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no God" (Isaiah 44:6); "before me there was no God formed, neither shall there be after me… beside me there is no saviour" (Isaiah 43:10–11). Whatever the Bible goes on to say about Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, it never takes one step back from this.

### The invisible God — and His image

"God is a Spirit" (John 4:24) — "eternal, immortal, invisible" (1 Timothy 1:17); "no man hath seen God at any time" (John 1:18). How, then, does an invisible God show Himself? The Scripture answers with the Word:

<Scripture ref="John 1:1–3">
  "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. The same was in the beginning with God. All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made."
</Scripture>

The Word — in John's Greek, the *Logos* — is not a second god beside God, and not a creature; John's whole point is that the Word *was* God: God's own self going forth into expression, the One by whom all things were made. Through Him the invisible God has an image:

<Scripture ref="Colossians 1:15">
  "Who is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of every creature:"
</Scripture>

<Scripture ref="Philippians 2:6–7">
  "Who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God: But made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant, and was made in the likeness of men:"
</Scripture>

Notice Scripture's own word: *form*. He was "in the form of God" before He took "the form of a servant" — "the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person" (Hebrews 1:3). And the Old Testament already knew God meeting men in a form: "the LORD appeared unto him in the plains of Mamre" (Genesis 18:1); Jacob named the place Peniel, "for I have seen God face to face" (Genesis 32:30). Before Bethlehem ever came, the invisible God had a way of stepping into sight — and Jesus reached back to it in prayer: "glorify thou me with thine own self with the glory which I had with thee before the world was" (John 17:5).

### The Word made flesh

Then came the step the whole Old Testament waited for:

<Scripture ref="John 1:14">
  "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth."
</Scripture>

His very name announced what had happened — "they shall call his name Emmanuel, which being interpreted is, God with us" (Matthew 1:23). Paul states it as plainly as language allows:

<Scripture ref="1 Timothy 3:16">
  "And without controversy great is the mystery of godliness: God was manifest in the flesh, justified in the Spirit, seen of angels, preached unto the Gentiles, believed on in the world, received up into glory."
</Scripture>

"God was in Christ, reconciling the world unto himself" (2 Corinthians 5:19); "for in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily" (Colossians 2:9) — the fulness, not a third of it. And when one of His own disciples asked to see the Father, Jesus gave the answer this whole teaching rests on:

<Scripture ref="John 14:8–9">
  "Philip saith unto him, Lord, shew us the Father, and it sufficeth us. Jesus saith unto him, Have I been so long time with you, and yet hast thou not known me, Philip? he that hath seen me hath seen the Father; and how sayest thou then, Shew us the Father?"
</Scripture>

"I and my Father are one" (John 10:30) — and He explained the relation Himself: "the Father that dwelleth in me, he doeth the works" (John 14:10). Not a second person standing beside the first: the eternal Spirit dwelling in His own manhood.

### One throne — and one face

Isaiah's "first and last" is the only God there is. In Revelation, the First and the Last speaks again — and says something only Jesus can say:

<Scripture ref="Revelation 1:17–18">
  "And when I saw him, I fell at his feet as dead. And he laid his right hand upon me, saying unto me, Fear not; I am the first and the last: I am he that liveth, and was dead; and, behold, I am alive for evermore, Amen; and have the keys of hell and of death."
</Scripture>

The God of Isaiah — who "was dead." One Person of the Godhead did not visit death while two watched: the First and the Last Himself was dead and is alive for evermore, "the Almighty" (Revelation 1:8). And when John is caught up to heaven, he does not see a committee: "behold, a throne was set in heaven, and one sat on the throne" (Revelation 4:2). In the city to come there is no temple, "for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are the temple of it… and the Lamb is the light thereof" (Revelation 21:22–23):

<Scripture ref="Revelation 22:3–4">
  "And there shall be no more curse: but the throne of God and of the Lamb shall be in it; and his servants shall serve him: And they shall see his face; and his name shall be in their foreheads."
</Scripture>

One throne — "his" throne. One face — "his" face. One name on every forehead. Eternity does not hold three enthroned persons; it holds one God, seen for ever "in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6).

## What Brother Branham Taught

Brother Branham told the Godhead the way Scripture unfolds it — not as a formula, but as a history: the eternal Spirit, then the Word, then the flesh. He would take his hearers back before the beginning:

<SermonQuote title="Questions and Answers on Genesis" date="July 29, 1953" ref="53-0729">
  "There's just space. There's no light, there's no dark, there's no nothing, it just seems nothing. But in there is a great supernatural Being, Jehovah God, who covered all space of all places of all times."
</SermonQuote>

There the eternal Spirit dwelt alone with His attributes — His thoughts: Father, Saviour, Healer, waiting to be expressed. He even noted that the word "God" means "an object of worship" — before creation there was nothing yet to worship Him (*Who Is This Melchisedec?*, 65-0221E). Then came the first step out of invisibility — the Spirit formed Himself a body:

<SermonQuote title="Who Is This Melchisedec?" date="February 21, 1965" ref="65-0221E">
  "His first being was Spirit, God, supernatural … the great eternal. Second, He begin to form Himself towards flesh, in a theophany it's called, 'the Word; a body.'"
</SermonQuote>

This is what he called the *Logos* — the theophany, the Word-body. Describing it, he said, "we see a little white Light forming out there … That was the Word of God" (53-0729). That Word was the One who said "Let there be light," who walked in the cool of the day in Eden, who sat at Abraham's tent door — God Himself come into a form, not a second god beside Him. And in the fulness of time, the same God took the last step down:

<SermonQuote title="Questions and Answers on Genesis" date="July 29, 1953" ref="53-0729">
  "'In the beginning was the Word, … and the Word was God.' 'And the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us.' God unfolding Himself, down to a human being."
</SermonQuote>

<SermonQuote title="Christ Is the Mystery of God Revealed" date="July 28, 1963" ref="63-0728">
  "All that God was, He poured into Christ, and all Christ was, was poured into the church."
</SermonQuote>

So Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, in his teaching, are not three eternal persons; they are three offices — three dispensations in which the one Spirit has revealed Himself — "three attributes of the same God," as he put it in the same 1963 sermon:

<SermonQuote title="The Godhead Explained" date="April 25, 1961" ref="61-0425B">
  "I do not believe that there is three individual gods. I believe there is one God in three offices: Father, Son, Holy Ghost."
</SermonQuote>

<SermonQuote title="The Godhead Explained" date="April 25, 1961" ref="61-0425B">
  "God above us; God with us; God in us. Not three gods — one God."
</SermonQuote>

God above His people in the Fatherhood, over Israel in the Pillar of Fire; God with His people in the Son, "Emmanuel"; God in His people now by the Holy Ghost — which is why Jesus could promise "another Comforter" and in the next breath say "I will come to you" (John 14:16–18), and why Paul writes, "now the Lord is that Spirit" (2 Corinthians 3:17). Same Spirit, three offices — never three persons.

And the journey does not run backward. The body God prepared, indwelt, raised, and glorified is His dwelling for ever: the Lamb on the throne. Brother Branham preached that the visible form of God — now and for all eternity — is Jesus Christ. That is why, as the [Water Baptism](/teachings/water-baptism/) page shows, he baptized in that Name; and it is why he refused both labels men offered him, "trinitarian" and "Jesus Only" alike, holding simply to what he found written: one God, manifest in the flesh.

## In Harmony with the Word

Two honest observations belong here. First, the word "trinity" never appears in Scripture; the word "Godhead" appears three times (Acts 17:29; Romans 1:20; Colossians 2:9), each time in the singular, and once located: "in him … bodily." The absence of a word does not by itself settle a doctrine — but it does mean the question must be settled by what the verses say, not by what the creeds assume.

Second, the Bible really does contain two families of texts, and both must stand. There are the absolute one-God texts — Deuteronomy 6:4, Isaiah 44:6 — and there are the relational texts: the Son praying to the Father, the voice from heaven, "my Father is greater than I." The familiar three-persons reading answers the second family of texts by placing distinct persons within God. This teaching answers it without that step: the distinction Scripture draws is between the eternal Spirit and the true manhood He begot and indwelt — and the reader may weigh which understanding lets both families of verses stand exactly as written. "The Father that dwelleth in me" (John 14:10); "God was in Christ" (2 Corinthians 5:19). The prayers were real — a true man praying to the true God — and the oneness was real: "he that hath seen me hath seen the Father." Even the King James Bible's strongest threefold verse ends by refusing the division: "and these three are one" (1 John 5:7) — and its middle term is not "the Son" but "the Word."

Read John 14 slowly and the whole teaching is there. This page claims nothing more than Deuteronomy 6:4 taken at full strength, with John 1:14 taken at full strength beside it.

## Common Questions

**At Jesus' baptism the Son stood in the water, the Spirit descended, and a voice spoke from heaven. Isn't that three persons in one scene?**

The scene is real, and the question is fair — it is the passage most often quoted for three persons (Matthew 3:16–17). But the Jordan was not where God and His Son first came together. The child was born Emmanuel, "God with us" (Matthew 1:23) — the Word was made flesh at Bethlehem, not at the river — and at twelve He already called the temple "my Father's business" (Luke 2:49). Watch, then, what Scripture says happened at the water: "God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Ghost and with power … for God was with him" (Acts 10:38). The descending Spirit was not a third party greeting a second, and not a reunion of two who had been apart; it was the public anointing of the Man God had begotten and indwelt from the manger — the Father sealing His Son (John 6:27) and showing Him openly to Israel (John 1:31–33). The dove and the voice did not begin the indwelling Jesus described — "the Father that dwelleth in me" — they crowned and completed it, the Spirit resting on Him without measure for the ministry now opening (John 3:34). One God, speaking and descending; one Son, standing in the water: Emmanuel from birth, anointed at Jordan.

**The Bible says Jesus was conceived of the Holy Ghost — yet He called God His Father. If the Father and the Holy Ghost are two persons, whose Son is He?**

Scripture is explicit about who begot the child: "she was found with child of the Holy Ghost" (Matthew 1:18); "that which is conceived in her is of the Holy Ghost" (Matthew 1:20). Yet the voice from heaven said, "This is my beloved Son" (Matthew 3:17), and Jesus called God His Father. If the Father and the Holy Ghost were two distinct persons, then the person who begot Him would not be the person He called Father — the record would give Him two fathers, and Scripture will not allow it. Gabriel's own sentence closes the gap, saying one thing twice over: "The Holy Ghost shall come upon thee, and the power of the Highest shall overshadow thee: therefore also that holy thing which shall be born of thee shall be called the Son of God" (Luke 1:35). The Holy Ghost is "the power of the Highest" — the Highest Himself, for "God is a Spirit" (John 4:24) and there is "one Spirit" (Ephesians 4:4). Brother Branham drew the only conclusion the verses leave open: "God the Father and God the Holy Ghost, as we often express these terms, make the Father and the Spirit ONE. Indeed they are, or else Jesus had two Fathers. But notice that Jesus said that He and His Father were One, not two. That makes ONE God" (*An Exposition of the Seven Church Ages*, Chapter One: "The Revelation of Jesus Christ"). So the conception proves what the baptism confirmed: Father and Holy Ghost are not two persons but one eternal Spirit — the same Spirit who begot the body, indwelt it, and had prepared it for Himself: "a body hast thou prepared me" (Hebrews 10:5).

**If Jesus is God, who was He praying to? And why did He say, "my Father is greater than I"?**

Because the manhood was real. The Son is not a costume; He is a true man — born, tired, hungry, tempted, and finally dying, which the eternal Spirit cannot do. As that man, He prayed, and as that man the Spirit was greater than He (John 14:28) — greater as the one who indwells is greater than the tent He dwells in. Yet the same lips said, "Before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58) and "I and my Father are one" (John 10:30). Both are true of the one Christ: God manifest in flesh — flesh that truly prayed, Spirit that truly answered.

**Why does Genesis say, "Let us make man in our image" (Genesis 1:26)?**

The verse names no second person; it must be read with the Scripture that tells us who was with God in the beginning: His own Word, by whom "all things were made" (John 1:1–3). Brother Branham taught that God was speaking to the Word — His own visible form, the theophany — the image after which man was patterned. Whatever one makes of the plural, it cannot be stretched into a council of co-equal persons without importing what no verse of the chapter says.

**Is this the same as the "Oneness" or "Jesus Only" teaching?**

It shares the confession that God is one and that His Name is Jesus Christ — but Brother Branham refused the "Jesus Only" label. He kept what that label tends to flatten: the real distinction between the eternal Spirit and the manhood He dwelt in, the real unfolding of one God through three offices across the ages, and (as the [Water Baptism](/teachings/water-baptism/) page notes) the full scriptural Name, *the Lord Jesus Christ*. He stood between the two camps and called both back to the same place: the Word.
